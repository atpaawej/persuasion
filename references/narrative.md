# Narrative transportation

## The mechanism

A well-structured story produces **narrative transportation**: the reader is mentally inside
the scene rather than evaluating the claim from outside it. Three effects follow:

1. **Counter-arguing drops.** Raw data invites System 2 to hunt for objections. A story gives
   the mind somewhere to be, and the objections do not get formed.
2. **Fluency reads as truth.** Stories arrive in a familiar shape — someone wanted something,
   something blocked it, something changed. Low processing cost is experienced as
   believability and safety.
3. **The conclusion travels with the emotion.** Transported readers adopt the stance of the
   story rather than deriving their own from the evidence.

This is a real advantage and a real hazard: the same mechanism carries a true claim and a
false one equally well. That is why the disclosure test is not optional.

## Arc 1 — Sparkline (Duarte)

Oscillate between two states to build tension, then resolve:

- **What is** — the present reality, with its specific cost.
- **What could be** — the transformed state.
- Alternate several times, each swing sharper than the last, and land on the **new bliss**:
  the permanent state after adoption.

Use for pitches, keynotes, and any "change the status quo" argument.

## Arc 2 — Hero's Guide (StoryBrand)

- **The audience is the hero.** The seller is not.
- **You are the guide** — equipped with empathy ("I know what this costs you") and authority
  ("I have taken others through it").
- **Give a plan of three steps**, name the stakes (what failure costs), and paint the success.

Use for landing pages, sales calls, and proposals. The most common failure is casting the
company as the hero of its own case study.

## Self-referential encoding

Information processed relative to the self is remembered better and adopted more readily.
Write the audience into the sentence: not "teams save time" but "you stop rebuilding the
same report every Monday." Second person, specific situation, their vocabulary.

## Structure a five-beat pitch

1. **A person in a specific situation** — enough detail that the reader recognises theirs.
2. **The obstacle** — named precisely, not generically.
3. **The turn** — the idea or product arrives as the thing that changed it.
4. **The evidence** — one number, one quote, one before/after; keep it short.
5. **The new state** — life after adoption, in the audience's terms, then the ask.

Keep the story one person deep. Two protagonists split the transportation.
