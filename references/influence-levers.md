# Influence levers

Seven principles, each with the mechanism underneath it. The mechanism is what you adapt;
the label is what you check yourself against.

## The seven

1. **Reciprocity.** Societies carry an internalised norm of repayment. Genuine, unsolicited
   value given first creates a sense of debt. The word *genuine* does the work: a "free"
   report that is really a sales brochure creates the opposite.
2. **Commitment and consistency.** People need their actions to match their stated positions
   and their self-image. Small public commitments predict larger ones. Get the first "yes"
   to be small, specific, and true.
3. **Social proof.** Under uncertainty, people infer the correct action from what similar
   others did. Fit is everything: proof from a different industry, size, or role reads as
   noise. Specific numbers from named, comparable customers.
4. **Authority.** Demonstrated expertise lowers decision anxiety and the need to verify.
   Credentials, cited work, and admitted limits all read as authority; confident assertion
   alone does not.
5. **Liking.** Influence flows through similarity, shared goals, and genuine regard. Find
   the real overlap; manufactured rapport is detectable.
6. **Scarcity.** Declining availability raises perceived value by activating loss aversion.
   True scarcity (a real deadline, a real cohort size) persuades. Invented scarcity is a red
   on the disclosure test and is usually caught.
7. **Unity.** Shared identity — "we are the same kind of person" — produces the strongest
   and most durable trust. It is built by naming the group's actual values, not by claiming
   membership.

Pair each lever to the diagnosis. Scarcity on a low-urgency, considered purchase reads as
pressure; authority on a peer-to-peer decision reads as distance.

## Biases that shape what gets believed

- **Availability.** Vivid, recent, emotionally charged examples are judged far more probable
  than base rates justify. One concrete story can outweigh a statistic — which is a reason to
  keep the story honest.
- **Representativeness.** Things that match a prototype are judged likely and high quality,
  ignoring base rates. This is why category conventions convert, and why a genuinely novel
  product needs an analogy before it needs a feature list.
- **Confirmation.** People seek confirming evidence and discount the rest. Build on the
  audience's existing model: agree with what is true in their position, then extend it. Direct
  contradiction triggers defence, not revision.

## Using these honestly

Every lever here describes how trust actually forms. None requires a lie:

- Reciprocity → give something worth having, with no string.
- Social proof → quote real customers whose situation matches the reader's.
- Scarcity → report a real limit, and be specific about what it is.
- Authority → show the work, including what you got wrong.

If a lever only works because the audience does not know it is being used, it fails the
disclosure test. See [ethics](ethics.md).
