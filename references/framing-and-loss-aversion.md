# Framing and loss aversion

## Reference-dependent valuation

People do not value outcomes in absolute terms. They evaluate them as gains or losses
against a **reference point**, usually the status quo, sometimes a prior price, a peer's
situation, or an expectation you set.

Litmus test for any offer: *against what baseline is this a win?* If you cannot name the
baseline, the audience will default to the status quo — and the status quo always wins ties.

## The asymmetry

The psychological value function is concave over gains and convex over losses, and steeper
in the loss domain. Losing $100 hurts roughly **1.5×–2.5×** as much as gaining $100 pleases.
Consequences:

- A feature framed as "you gain X" is weighed less than the same feature framed as "you stop
  losing X."
- A discount and a surcharge of equal size are not equal: the surcharge looms.
- People will pay to avoid a loss they would not pay to obtain an equivalent gain — which is
  why insurance, warranties, and guarantees convert.

## The fourfold pattern

| Outcome | Probability | Behaviour | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gain | High | Risk-averse | Take the sure $100 |
| Loss | High | Risk-seeking | Gamble to avoid a certain loss |
| Gain | Low | Risk-seeking | Buy the lottery ticket |
| Loss | Low | Risk-averse | Buy the insurance |

Use it to predict what the audience will do before you write the offer: a high-probability
gain gets accepted if it is certain; a high-probability loss gets fought, not accepted.

## Gain frame or loss frame

| | Gain frame | Loss frame |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Language | "Save", "gain", "reach", "double" | "Stop losing", "prevent", "avoid", "escape" |
| Fit | Approach-oriented audience, novel choice, low risk, upside-seeking | Defending what they have, prevention focus, urgent, already-aware problem |
| Failure mode | Feels optional; competes with inertia | Feels like a threat; triggers avoidance of the message itself |

Choose by the audience's focus, not by what sounds punchier. Then keep it consistent — mixing
frames inside one asset cancels both.

## Anchoring

The first number in the room sets the baseline, and later estimates adjust insufficiently
from it. Sequence accordingly: the expensive comparison, the cost of the problem, or the
full-price tier comes first; the actual number lands against it.

Honest anchoring uses real numbers you can defend. A fabricated "was $999" is a red on the
disclosure test, not a clever anchor.

## Endowment and status quo

People overvalue what they already hold, and non-adoption feels like a loss. Create
ownership before asking for the decision: a working demo with their data, a personalised
preview, a trial they configured themselves, a draft with their name on it. Then the ask is
not "acquire this" but "keep this."

## Applied recipe

1. Name the reference point out loud in the draft.
2. Decide gain or loss by the audience's current focus.
3. Put the anchor first and the real number second.
4. Build ownership before the ask, where the product allows it.
5. Check the frame survives one reading by someone who is not you.
